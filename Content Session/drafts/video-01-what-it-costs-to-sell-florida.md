## SCRIPT

**[1. HOOK]**
You spent years building your business. Before you sell it, you should know how much of the sale price actually ends up in your pocket. Because it's never the whole number.

I'm Ryan Winter, business broker with Truforte Business Group here in Florida. Let's walk through what it really costs to sell a business in this state, one line at a time.

**[2. THE BROKER]**
First, the broker. For most small and mid-sized businesses, the fee is a percentage of the sale price. It's paid at closing, and only if the business actually sells. On smaller deals, around ten percent is common. As deals get bigger, the percentage usually comes down. A good broker earns that fee by getting you a better price and a deal that actually closes.

**[3. YOUR ADVISORS]**
Second, your advisors. You want a business attorney reviewing the purchase agreement and closing documents, and a CPA planning your taxes before you sign anything. On a smaller deal, budget a few thousand dollars for each. On a bigger or more complicated deal, more. This is not the place to save money.

**[4. TAXES]**
Third, and usually the biggest one: taxes.

Here's the good news. Florida has no state personal income tax. So if your business is an LLC, an S corporation, or a sole proprietorship, the state doesn't take a cut of your gain. Compared to selling in most other states, that can be worth tens of thousands of dollars. One exception: if your company is a C corporation, Florida's corporate income tax can still apply.

The federal government still gets its share. Much of your gain is usually taxed at long-term capital gains rates. But some of it can be taxed as ordinary income, like depreciation you've already taken on your equipment. How the price gets split between equipment, goodwill, and things like a non-compete changes your tax bill, sometimes by a lot. That split is negotiated. So negotiate it with your CPA in the room.

**[5. FLORIDA-SPECIFIC COSTS]**
Fourth, two costs that are specific to Florida.

If your real estate is part of the sale, Florida charges documentary stamp tax on the deed. In most of the state that's seventy cents for every hundred dollars of the price, and the seller customarily pays it.

And sales tax. In Florida, a buyer can be held responsible for a seller's unpaid state sales tax. So expect the buyer to want proof you're current with the Department of Revenue, or to hold back part of your money at closing until you do. Clean that up before you list, not at the closing table.

**[6. THE COSTS NOBODY LISTS]**
Fifth, the costs nobody puts on the list. Paying off equipment loans and lines of credit at closing. A landlord who charges a fee to transfer your lease. Weeks or months training the new owner. And part of your price that may be paid later, through seller financing or an earnout, which means you carry some risk after you've handed over the keys.

**[7. THE REAL TAKEAWAY]**
Add it all up and the costs are real. But the most expensive mistake isn't anything on this list. It's selling for less than your business is worth because you went to market unprepared.

I'm Ryan Winter, business broker with Truforte Business Group. Thanks for watching.

## FACT CHECK NOTES (for Ryan)

- "Around ten percent is common" on smaller deals: confirm this matches how you want to talk about fees publicly.
- No Florida personal income tax: correct. Florida corporate income tax applies to C corporations.
- Doc stamp tax on deeds: $0.70 per $100 statewide; Miami-Dade uses a different rate, hence "in most of the state". Seller paying is custom, not law, and can be negotiated.
- Buyer liability for seller's unpaid sales tax: Florida Statutes section 213.758 (buyer must withhold enough to cover it unless the seller shows proof from the Department of Revenue).
- No specific dollar totals for taxes are stated on purpose, since they vary by deal structure.
